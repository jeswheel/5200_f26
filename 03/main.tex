\section{Completely Randomized Designs}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Completely Randomized Design}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Completely randomized design (CRD) is the simplest design for comparing treatments and is sometimes the preferred design. 
    \item \alert{Setup:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item There are $g$ treatment groups.
      \item $N$ experimental units (EU)s available.
      \item You want sample sizes of $n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_g$, with $\sum_{i = 1}^g n_i = N$. 
    \end{itemize}
  
  \framebreak
  
    \item \alert{Idea}: All possible sub-group composition are equally as likely. 
  \end{itemize}
  
  \begin{block}{Performing a Completely Randomized Design}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Randomly choose $n_1$ EU's for treatment $1$.
      \item Randomly choose $n_2$ EU's for treatment $2$.
      \item $\ldots$
      \item Randomly choose $n_{g-1}$ EU's for treatment $g-1$.
      \item The remaining $n_g$ EU's are assigned to treatment $g$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}
  
  \begin{itemize}
    \item On a computer, this can be done by permuting $1, 2, \ldots, N$, and assigning the first $n_1$ numbers to treatment 1, the next $n_2$ to treatment 2, etc.
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Advantages and Disadvantages}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Advantages
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Simple to do; all you need is one random permutation.
      \item Simple to explain and understand.
      \item Simple to analyze.
      \item No restrictions on the sample sizes (other designs may restrict $n_i$).
      \item Easy with missing values.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Disadvantages
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Not always possible or cost-effective.
      \item The accuracy of the experiment depends on variability among the EUs, not just the variability among treatment groups. Thus, it may require more EUs (\$\$\$\$).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Example: Acid Rain}
  \cite{wood74} studied the effect of acid rain on trees. 
  One experiment used 240 six-week-old yellow birch seedlings. These seedlings were divided into five groups of 48 \emph{at random}, and the seedlings within each group received an acid mist treatment 6 hours a week for 17 weeks.
  The five treatments differed by mist pH: 4.7, 4.0, 3.3, 3.0, and 2.3. Othwerwise, the seedlings were treated identically.
  After the 17 weeks, the seedlings were weighed, and total plant (dry) weight was taken as response.
  
  \textbf{Questions:}
  
  \begin{itemize}
    \item What type of design is this, and can you describe why?
    \item What are the EUs, and how many are there?
    \item What was the response?
  
  \framebreak
    \item What compromises may have been made in this case?
    
    \mode<article>{
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Damage may vary by pH level, plant developmental stage, plant species, etc., yet only pH level was directly addressed.
        \item The study wishes to study the impact of acid-rain, but the available treatment was a synthetic acid rain, which may have a slightly different impact.
        \item Total plant weight is an important response, but other responses measuring tree health may also be available.
      \end{itemize}
        }
        
        \mode<presentation>{\vspace{2cm}}
        
        Like many experiments, the researchers necessarily have to narrow down an enormous question to a workable experiment using artificial acid rain under controlled conditions.
        For any experiment, a considerable amount of non-statistical background work goes into the planning even the simplest experiment. 
  
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Models and Parameters}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Introduction and definitions}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item All formal statistical analysis is based on probability models.
    \item \emph{EX:} The number of heads in ten tosses of a fair coin would be Binomial$(10, 0.5)$. Here, the model depends on 2 parameters: $N = 10$ the number of trials, $p = 0.5$ is the probability of heads.
    \item In an experiment, we may posit several different models for the data, all with \emph{unknown} parameters. 
    One of our objectives is often to describe which model is the best description of the data, and make \emph{inference} about parameters.
  \end{itemize}
  
  \begin{block}{Definition: Inference}
    Using data to learn properties of a probability distribution. In our case, using data to estimate parameters of a model.
  \end{block}
  
  \framebreak
  
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The models we will consider for experimental design will have two basic parts:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item A description of the expected value, or mean, of the data. Sometimes called ``model for the means".
      \item A description of how data vary around the treatment means. Sometimes called `model for the errors". 
    \end{itemize}
    \item For the errors, we will generally assume that they are independent for different data values, have mean zero, and all have a common variance $\sigma^2$.
    \item To perform formal tests and obtain confidence intervals, we will further assume a distribution of the errors.
    Generally, we assume that the errors follow a \alert{Gaussian} (Normal) distribution.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}
