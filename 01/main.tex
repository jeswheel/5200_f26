\section{Course Introduction}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Course Overview}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item This class is all about developing techniques for answering scientific questions.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Is a newly proposed drug a safe and effective cure for a disease?
      \item Which combination of protein and carbohydrate sources provide the best nutrition for growing lambs?
      \item How will long-distance telephone usage change if our company offers a different rate structure to our customers?
    \end{itemize}
    \item Our goal is to help decision makers and researchers design good experiments, analyze them properly, and answer their research questions.
    \item The course is based on the textbook ``A First Course in Design and Analysis of Experiments" \citep{oehlert2000}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Experiments}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{What is an ``Experiment" and why it matters}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Experiments are the gold-standard for scientific inference.
    \item Origins of the design a study of experiments starting with R. A. Fisher in the 1920s--1940s at UK agricultural center.
    \item His work developed the field of experimental design, which is used now in all forms of scientific investigations.
    \item Large advances in medicine, science, and product quality improvements can be attributed to proper experimental design and analysis.
  \end{itemize}
    \framebreak
    
    \begin{exampleblock}{Fisher's early examples}
      Fisher found decades worth of fertilizer treatment data: 
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Fertilizer had been applied to a field one year, and not the next, in order to compare yield of grain produced in the two different years.
        \item Fertilizer was applied to one field in one year, and not to a nearby field in the same year, again to compare the yield of grain produced in the same year.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{exampleblock}
    
    \framebreak
    
    From a statistical perspective, there are three components that define an experiment: \alert{Treatments}, \alert{Experimental Units}, and \alert{Assignment Method}.
   
   \begin{block}{Experiment}
    An \alert{experiment} is specified by the combination of treatments, experimental units, and assignment methods.
   \end{block}
   
   \begin{itemize}
    \item \alert{Note:} There is no mention of a \emph{method} for analyzing the results of an experiment. Strictly speaking, the analysis is not part of the design, though a wise experimenter will consider the analysis when planning an experiment.
   \end{itemize}
    
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Treatments}
  
  \mode<article>{
    \begin{itemize} 
      \item \emph{Treatments} are the different procedures we want to compare.
      \begin{itemize}
        \item There can be more than one treatment.
        \item Each individual treatment is possibly determined by several \emph{factors}.
        \item Treatments can be either categorical or numerical.
      \end{itemize}
      \item \textbf{EX:} In a fertilizer formula, one gets to choose whether or not to include one of 20 chemical additives. How many possible treatments are there?
      \vspace{3cm}
      \item \textbf{EX:} The baking treatment for a cake involves a given time at a given temperature. Considering 5 baking times and 5 temperatures, how many possible treatments can one include in an experiment?
      \vspace{3cm}
    \end{itemize}
  }
  
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Experimental Units}
  
  \mode<article>{
    \begin{itemize} 
      \item \emph{Experimental Units} (EU) are the entities which a treatment will be applied. Examples:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Plots of land receiving fertilizers.
        \item Pens containing many pigs or other animals all getting the same diet (\emph{Individual animal is NOT an EU}).
        \item Classroom containing many students, all being taught the same way (Individual student is \emph{NOT} an EU).
        \item Individual patients in a clinic getting personalized treatments.
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{3cm}
    \end{itemize}
  }
  
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Assignment Methods}
  
  \mode<article>{
    \begin{itemize} 
      \item \emph{Assignment Method} defines how treatments are assigned to units (or units assigned to treatments). Examples (some good, some bad):
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Assign fertilizer one year, not the next, to the same plot of land.
        \item Split a single field into several sub-plots, and randomly pick subplots to apply fertilizer. Repeat this over multiple years. 
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{3cm}
    \end{itemize}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Measurement Units vs Experimental Units}
  
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A common source of difficulty and confusion is the distinction between \emph{experimental units} and \emph{measurement units}.
  \end{itemize}
  
  \begin{block}{Definition: Measurement unit}
    A measurement unit is the individual or piece of experiment material you actually measure.
  \end{block}
  
  
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Consider an educational study, where 6 classrooms of first graders are assigned at random to two different reading programs, and all first graders are later evaluated via a common reading exam at the end of the year.
    
    \mode<article>{\vspace{3cm}}
    
      \framebreak
    
    \item In an animal nutrition experiment, different types of diets are given to pens that each contain many individual animals.
    
        \mode<article>{\vspace{3cm}}

  \end{itemize}
  
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{High-level and Low-level EU’s}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The distinction between experimental units and measurement units can become complicated.
    \item For example, in split-plot experiments (discussed later), there can be multiple layers of comparisons, with two or more types of experimental units with \alert{low-level EUs}, serving as measurement units for the \alert{high-level EUs}.
  \end{itemize}
  
  \mode<presentation>{(Example on next slide)}
  
  \framebreak
  
  \begin{exampleblock}{Baking experiment}
    A baking experiment is used to study the effect of high and low levels of three ingredients in a recipe ($2^3 = 8$ possible combinations), and the effect of baking temperature (6 different levels). You have an oven that can bake 8 batches at one time, at one temperature.
    
    In the experiment, you run the oven 6 times, each with 8 batches made up of the 8 combinations of ingredients randomly placed in the oven. 
  \end{exampleblock}
  
  \textbf{What are the treatments, experimental units, and measurement units?}
  
  \mode<article>{
  
    \vspace{1cm}
    
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The Treatments are temperature (6 levels, we call it a high-level treatment), and recipe (combinations of ingredients, a low-level treatment).
      \item The \alert{high-level EUs} are the ``runs" of the oven at a given temperature.
      \item The \alert{low-level EUs} are the individual ``batches" with unique ingredients that make up the 8 combinations.
      \item The \alert{measurement units} are the 8 batches in an oven (the thing we measure), which is the same as the low-level EUs.
    \end{itemize}
  }
  
\end{frame}

\section{Observational studies vs experiments}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Observational studies vs experiments}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Experiments help answer questions, but there are also non-experimental techniques. What makes experiments special?
    \item The most important feature is that an experiment gives researchers control: we get to pick the treatment and assignments.
    \item This is different from \emph{observational studies}, where there are also treatments, units, and responses, but the researcher does not get to control treatment assignment.
    \framebreak
    \item If an experiment is designed well, we can
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Directly compare impact of various treatments, minimizing \emph{confounding}.
      \item Design experiments to minimize any form of \emph{bias}.
      \item Design experiments so that the \emph{error} in comparison is small.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Design considerations}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Design issues and considerations}
  \begin{itemize} 
    \item An important feature of an experiment is that the units are \alert{independent}, meaning that the response of one unit is \emph{not} influenced by treatments or responses of other units.
    \item We should also consider \alert{edge effects}, which can occur in both space and time:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{EX (Space):} Plants at the edge of a plot or a greenhouse may respond differently to treatments.
      \item \textbf{EX (Time):} Flushing out old treatments, or applying treatments at certain time of day.
      \item We should account for these possibilities by ensuring sufficient separation of units, and provisioning a ``buffer" in both space and time. 
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Fixed resource problem}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item An often-encountered design problem is when there is a fixed amount of resource available for experimenting.
    \item Examples:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Fixed sized field
        \item Fixed amount of time
        \item Fixed amount of money to spend on experiment.
      \end{itemize}
    \item In these situations, a key part of the problem include: Size / shape of plots, number of samples per session, etc.
    \item \alert{Useful rule of thumb:} Do \emph{more}, less well. That is, have more experimental units instead of more measurement units. 
    
    \mode<article>{
    \vspace{1cm}
    \textbf{EX:} In a comparison of teaching methods, it may be better to study 1 classroom in each of 20 schools (20 EUs, 20 measurement units), instead of 5 classrooms in 4 schools (4 EUs, still 20 measurement units). The trade-off here is that the first approach has more replicates, but higher-variance amongst EUs; the second approach has fewer replicates, but also lower variance in each replicate. Often the first approach will yield better final results.
    }
    
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
